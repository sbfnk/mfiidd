Choosing an inference approach

Which method does your own problem need?

Estimated time: 45 minutes | Closing session | Requires: all taught sessions

Lecture slides

Introduction

You have now met every family of method this course covers. This closing session puts them side by side and asks the question you will actually face when you go back to your own work: given this model and this data, which one do I reach for?

There is no algorithm for the answer. But the choice is much less open than it looks, because two properties of your problem rule most of the options out before you start.

The two questions that decide it

Important
  1. Can you write down and evaluate \(p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\)?
  2. Can you differentiate it?

Everything else is detail. The first question separates likelihood-based methods from simulation-based ones. The second separates gradient-based samplers from everything else, and it is the question people most often fail to ask before choosing a tool.

A deterministic model with a parametric observation model answers yes to both, which is why the introduction and MCMC sessions could go straight to NUTS. A stochastic model answers no to the first, because the likelihood involves an integral over every trajectory the model could have taken, which is what sent us to particle filters. A model you can only simulate answers no to both, which is ABC.

The methods, side by side

Method Needs Cost Reach for it when
Grid search likelihood hopeless beyond 2-3 parameters you want to see the posterior
Metropolis-Hastings likelihood cheap per step, many steps the model is small, or you need to understand what a sampler does
RAM / adaptive MH likelihood as MH, tunes itself MH mixes badly and you cannot differentiate
NUTS likelihood and gradient expensive per step, few steps you can differentiate: almost always the default
Particle filter + PMMH simulator of a stochastic model very expensive the state is stochastic and you need the parameters
ABC simulator only expensive, and approximate you cannot write a likelihood at all

Variational inference sits outside this ordering: it needs everything NUTS needs, and trades accuracy for speed rather than trading assumptions for cost. Reach for it when you need an answer in seconds and can accept a biased posterior.

Warning

The table reads top to bottom as needing less of you, which is what lets it cope with harder models. That generality is not free. Every step down it buys you the ability to handle a harder model by spending more computation, accepting more approximation, or both. Do not take a step you do not need: fitting a deterministic model with ABC is not wrong, it is merely thousands of times slower than fitting it with NUTS and less accurate.

TipExercise: match the problem to the method

For each of these, decide which method you would try first and, more importantly, why the ones above it in the table will not do.

  1. An SEIR model fitted to weekly case counts, five parameters, deterministic.
  2. The same model, but the transmission rate is a random walk over 100 weeks.
  3. An individual-based simulation of household transmission, where the likelihood would require summing over every possible chain of infection.
  4. A deterministic model you need to refit every hour as new data arrive.
  5. A stochastic compartmental model with six parameters and a small population, where demographic noise genuinely matters.
TipSolution
  1. NUTS. Deterministic, few parameters, differentiable.
  2. Still NUTS, but the 100 random-walk increments are parameters too, so this is a 105-dimensional problem and the AD backend becomes a real decision. See Beyond Turing on forward versus reverse mode.
  3. ABC, or a synthetic likelihood. You can simulate households easily and cannot write the likelihood at all.
  4. Variational inference, if you can tolerate the bias, and NUTS if you can afford it. This is the one case where “fast and slightly wrong” beats “slow and right”, because an answer after the decision is worth nothing.
  5. Particle MCMC. The state is stochastic, so the likelihood needs marginalising over trajectories, which is exactly what the particle filter estimates.

Two things the map leaves out

States, not just parameters. The SEITL session framed the posterior as

\[p(x, \theta \mid y) = p(\theta \mid y)\, p(x \mid \theta, y)\]

Everything above is about the first factor. The second is state estimation: what the epidemic was actually doing, as opposed to which parameters generated it. A particle filter gives you both, which is part of why it is worth its cost.

Comparing models, not just fitting one. Whether SEITL or SEIT4L describes Tristan da Cunha better is a question no amount of sampling a single model answers. Fit both and compare them, on predictive performance or on the marginal likelihood where you can get at it. The PMCMC session does exactly this.

What actually goes wrong

In practice the method is rarely the thing that fails. Four other things are.

The observation model. A Poisson likelihood on overdispersed data makes the posterior far too confident and lets one bad day dominate the fit, as the observation models session showed. This is the most common real failure, and no amount of sampler sophistication rescues it.

The priors. Vague priors are not neutral. A Uniform(1, 20) on \(R_0\) says a value of 19 is as plausible as 2, and prior predictive checks turn that from an abstraction into a picture of epidemics that infect everyone in four days.

Identifiability. If two parameters trade off against each other, the data cannot separate them and no sampler will either. This is a property of the model and the data rather than of the algorithm, which is why throwing a better sampler at it changes nothing. The diagnostics session is about noticing the symptoms.

The model. A model that cannot produce the data you observed will not be saved by better inference. Posterior predictive checks are how you find out.

Important

Sampler problems announce themselves: R̂, divergences, terrible trace plots. Model problems do not. A converged chain from a bad model looks exactly like a converged chain from a good one, which is why model checking is a separate discipline from diagnostics.

Beyond this course

The methods here are the working set, and there are two directions people commonly go next.

Scale. Fitting many models, or one model to many regions, means parallel chains, GPUs, and tools built for it. The Julia stack is well placed here: sample(model, NUTS(), MCMCThreads(), n, n_chains) is the entire change needed to run chains in parallel, and the wider ecosystem session covers sampling outside Turing when you need it.

Structure. Real surveillance data arrives late, incompletely, and through several different streams at once. Reporting delays, nowcasting and multi-source evidence synthesis all sit on top of what you have learned rather than replacing it: the model gets more elaborate, the inference machinery stays the same.

TipExercise: your own problem

Take a model you actually want to fit.

  1. Can you write down \(p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\)? If not, what is stopping you: a stochastic state, an intractable sum, or a simulator you did not write?
  2. Can you differentiate it? If the model calls out to code you cannot differentiate through, that rules out NUTS regardless of everything else.
  3. Which row of the table does that leave you on?
  4. What would you check first if the answer looked wrong?

If you have time, discuss it with the person next to you. Question 4 is usually the one that changes people’s plans.

TipLearning points
  • Two questions decide most of it: can you evaluate the likelihood, and can you differentiate it
  • Generality costs computation and accuracy, so take the most specific method your problem allows
  • The observation model, the priors and identifiability cause more real failures than the choice of sampler
  • Sampler problems are loud and model problems are silent, which is why diagnostics and model checking are separate jobs
