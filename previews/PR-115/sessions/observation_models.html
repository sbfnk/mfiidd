Observation models

Choosing how your model meets your data

Estimated time: 55 minutes | Requires: MCMC

Slides

Introduction

Every likelihood in this course so far has assumed that observed case counts follow a Poisson distribution:

\[\text{obs}_t \sim \text{Poisson}(\rho \cdot I_t)\]

That is a modelling choice, and it makes a specific claim about how observation works. This session asks whether the claim holds, what happens when it does not, and what a likelihood is really doing when you fit with one.

Objectives

In this session you will:

  1. Critically evaluate observation model choices (Poisson, negative binomial)
  2. Check whether the Poisson variance assumption holds for real data
  3. Understand negative log-likelihood as an implicit distance metric
  4. Make informed choices about observation models for your own research

Simple SIR model for comparison

For this session, let’s use a simpler SIR model to make the concepts clearer:

"""
Simple SIR model for pedagogical purposes.
"""
function simulate_sir(θ, init_state, times)
    R_0, D_inf = θ[:R_0], θ[:D_inf]

    function sir_ode!(du, u, p, t)
        S, I, R = u
        β, γ = p
        N = S + I + R

        du[1] = -β * S * I / N
        du[2] = β * S * I / N - γ * I
        du[3] = γ * I
    end

    β = R_0 / D_inf
    γ = 1.0 / D_inf
    u0 = [init_state[:S], init_state[:I], init_state[:R]]
    prob = ODEProblem(sir_ode!, u0, (times[1], times[end]), [β, γ])
    sol = solve(prob, Tsit5(), saveat=times)

    # Extract incidence (new infections per day)
    S_values = sol[1, :]
    incidence = [0.0; -diff(S_values)]

    return DataFrame(time=times, S=S_values,
                     I=sol[2, :],
                     R=sol[3, :],
                     Inc=incidence)
end

What we have used so far

This is what we’ve been doing throughout the course:

@model function sir_poisson(times, init_state, n_obs)
    # Priors
    R_0 ~ Uniform(1.0, 20.0)
    D_inf ~ Uniform(1.0, 10.0)
    ρ ~ Uniform(0.1, 1.0)  # Reporting rate

    # Simulate
    θ = Dict(:R_0 => R_0, :D_inf => D_inf)
    traj = simulate_sir(θ, init_state, times)

    # Likelihood: Poisson observation model
    lambdas = [max(ρ * traj.Inc[i + 1], 1e-10) for i in 1:n_obs]
    obs ~ arraydist(Poisson.(lambdas))

    return traj
end

NoteReminder: arraydist and | conditioning

obs ~ arraydist(Poisson.(lambdas)) declares a vector of independent Poisson observations, one per time point. The | operator, applied when creating the model instance below, conditions the model on the actual data. See the MCMC session for a full explanation of this pattern.

What does Poisson assume?

The Poisson distribution has a key property: variance = mean

# Illustrate Poisson assumption
λ_values = [5, 20, 50]
p = plot(title="Poisson Distribution: Variance = Mean",
         xlabel="Count", ylabel="Probability")

for λ in λ_values
    dist = Poisson(λ)
    x_range = max(0, λ-20):min(λ+20, λ*2)
    plot!(p, x_range, [pdf(dist, x) for x in x_range],
          label="λ=$λ (σ²=$λ)", linewidth=2)
end

TipExercise: Does the assumption hold for our data?

The Poisson model claims the variance of the observations equals their mean. Check whether that is plausible for the Tristan da Cunha data.

  1. Fit the Poisson model above and extract the posterior mean trajectory.
  2. Compute the residuals (observed minus predicted) and their variance.
  3. Compare that variance against the mean prediction. If the data were Poisson, the two should be roughly equal.

What do you find, and what would it imply for the parameter estimates?

# Residual variance against mean prediction, under the Poisson fit
θ_fit = Dict(:R_0 => mean(chain_poisson[:R_0]), :D_inf => mean(chain_poisson[:D_inf]))
pred_fit = mean(chain_poisson[:ρ]) .* simulate_sir(θ_fit, init_test, times).Inc[2:end]
resid_fit = flu_tdc.obs .- pred_fit

println("mean prediction:    ", round(mean(pred_fit), digits = 2))
println("residual variance:  ", round(var(resid_fit), digits = 1))
println("ratio:              ", round(var(resid_fit) / mean(pred_fit), digits = 1),
        "  (Poisson demands 1)")
mean prediction:    3.58
residual variance:  87.0
ratio:              24.3  (Poisson demands 1)
TipSolution

The ratio printed above is around 25, where Poisson demands 1. The data are wildly overdispersed relative to Poisson.

Two things produce that, and it is worth keeping them apart. Some of it is genuine observation noise: two islanders infected in the same household are not independent draws, and reporting was not uniform across the outbreak. Most of it here is something else, namely that a single-wave SIR fitted to a two-wave outbreak is wrong about the mean, and everything it gets wrong about the mean lands in the residuals.

Either way the consequence for the Poisson fit is the same. It treats every departure from the trajectory as strong evidence against it, so the posterior comes out far too narrow. Nor can it buy its way out by moving the parameters: the four days around the observed peak are underpredicted by factors of about four to nine and stay that way, because the many low-count days of the second wave outweigh them. The negative binomial in the next section loosens that grip, but notice that it does so by explaining the misfit as noise rather than by fixing the model.

Reasons the assumption might fail for epidemic data:

  • Under-reporting might be systematic rather than random
  • Behavioural changes might create clustering
  • Surveillance quality might vary over time
TipExercise: Predict what the negative binomial will change

Before you fit it, commit to an answer for each of these. Write them down — the point is to be caught out if you are wrong.

  1. Will the posterior for \(R_0\) get wider, narrower, or stay about the same?
  2. Will the posterior mean of \(R_0\) move much?
  3. The model adds one parameter, \(\phi\). If the data were genuinely Poisson, what value would you expect the posterior for \(\phi\) to favour?

Then fit it below and check.

TipSolution

  1. Wider, and by a lot. The negative binomial can attribute a departure from the trajectory to observation noise, so the data pin the parameters down less tightly. Here the posterior standard deviation for \(R_0\) roughly doubles. Loosening the observation model transfers uncertainty back to the parameters, which is the honest direction: the Poisson intervals were narrow because the model was forbidden from admitting the data were noisy.

  2. The mean moves too, and that is the surprise. \(R_0\) goes from about 1.9 to about 2.7, several Poisson standard deviations apart. The two models do not merely disagree about the uncertainty, they disagree about the answer.

    The misfit sits almost entirely in the first wave. Days 6 to 9 carry about three quarters of the squared residual, and day 7 alone about a third: 47 cases reported against about five predicted. The fitted trajectory accounts for about 211 of the 312 reported cases, and two thirds of that shortfall falls in the first eighteen days, which carry about 90% of the squared residual. The Poisson fit cannot lift its peak to meet those days, because the outbreak has a second wave and a single SIR curve has only one peak to spend. Days 19 to 59 carry 109 of the 312 cases and rise again to 12 on day 32. Raising \(R_0\) makes the epidemic burn out sooner: at \(R_0 = 7\), holding \(D_\mathrm{inf}\) at its fitted value, the trajectory peaks on day 3 and predicts almost no cases at all after day 19, where the Poisson likelihood then gives those 109 observed cases essentially no probability. Fitting the same model to the first eighteen days alone removes the second wave, and \(R_0\) comes out around 7.

    The negative binomial adds a dispersion parameter, so departures from its mean cost much less: over days 19 to 59 its log-likelihood is about 54 units better than the Poisson’s. Less bound to track every day exactly, it settles on a longer infectious period and a larger \(R_0\), and so on a larger predicted total, about 245 cases against 211.

    Look at \(\rho\) in the comparison table below. The Poisson fit pushes it to about 0.99, against the upper bound of its Uniform(0.1, 1.0) prior. A parameter pinned to the edge of its prior is a model straining against a constraint, and here it is straining for the same reason. Even reporting everybody would not be enough: the model starts with 279 susceptibles, so it can never produce more than 279 cases against the 312 observed. The reporting rate is pinned at a bound that still cannot close the gap, which is about as clear a sign as this session offers that the shortfall is structural.

  3. Large \(\phi\) is the right answer to the question as asked, because variance is \(\mu + \mu^2/\phi\) and the second term vanishes as \(\phi \to \infty\). The data say the opposite: the posterior for \(\phi\) sits near 0.5, so the variance is roughly \(\mu + 2\mu^2\) and the extra flexibility was very much needed.

    Note that a small \(\phi\) is not proof of messy reporting. A single-wave SIR cannot reproduce a two-wave outbreak, and the negative binomial is absorbing that structural failure as though it were noise. An observation model will always volunteer to explain a misspecified mean, which is why the model checking session comes first.

The negative binomial likelihood

Allowing for overdispersion

The negative binomial adds a second parameter, so the variance is free to exceed the mean. For epidemic case counts that is the common situation rather than the exception, which makes this the more usual starting point in practice:

\[\text{obs}_t \sim \text{NegativeBinomial}(\mu_t, \phi)\]

where:

  • \(\mu_t = \rho \cdot I_t\) (mean)
  • \(\phi\) controls overdispersion (variance = \(\mu + \mu^2/\phi\))
@model function sir_negbin(times, init_state, n_obs)
    # Priors
    R_0 ~ Uniform(1.0, 20.0)
    D_inf ~ Uniform(1.0, 10.0)
    ρ ~ Uniform(0.1, 1.0)
    φ ~ Exponential(10.0)  # Overdispersion parameter

    # Simulate
    θ = Dict(:R_0 => R_0, :D_inf => D_inf)
    traj = simulate_sir(θ, init_state, times)

    # Likelihood: Negative Binomial observation model
    mus = [max(ρ * traj.Inc[i + 1], 1e-10) for i in 1:n_obs]
    # NegativeBinomial(r, p) where r=φ, p=φ/(φ+μ)
    obs ~ arraydist([NegativeBinomial(φ, φ/(φ+μ)) for μ in mus])

    return traj
end
sir_negbin (generic function with 2 methods)
TipCode explainer
  • NegativeBinomial(r, p) - Julia’s parameterisation uses r (number of successes) and p (success probability). To get mean μ with overdispersion φ, use r=φ and p=φ/(φ+μ). This gives variance = μ + μ²/φ, so larger φ approaches Poisson, smaller φ gives more overdispersion.

Honest question: Why Negative Binomial?

  • Mechanistic justification: Weak (no clear biological process)
  • Empirical justification: Fits better than Poisson
  • Convenience: Easy to compute, works with MCMC

Is this really different from choosing a distance metric?

Interlude: Likelihood as Distance

Likelihood as distance

The negative log-likelihood is actually a distance function between observed and predicted data:

\[\text{Distance} = -\log p(\text{obs} | \text{predicted})\]

We can see what distance each likelihood implies:

# Compare different observation models as distance functions
obs_val = 10.0
predicted_range = 1.0:0.5:20.0

# Poisson: -log P(obs | λ)
poisson_distance = [-logpdf(Poisson(λ), obs_val) for λ in predicted_range]

# Negative Binomial with different φ (convert to r,p parametrization)
# NegativeBinomial(r, p) where r=φ, p=φ/(φ+μ)
negbin_distance_phi5 = [-logpdf(NegativeBinomial(5.0, 5.0/(5.0+λ)), obs_val) for λ in predicted_range]
negbin_distance_phi20 = [-logpdf(NegativeBinomial(20.0, 20.0/(20.0+λ)), obs_val) for λ in predicted_range]

# Gaussian (for comparison)
gaussian_distance = [-logpdf(Normal(λ, 3.0), obs_val) for λ in predicted_range]

# Plot
plot(predicted_range, poisson_distance,
     label="Poisson", linewidth=2,
     xlabel="Predicted value", ylabel="-log(likelihood)",
     title="Different Likelihoods as Distance Functions (obs=10)",
     legend=:topright)
plot!(predicted_range, negbin_distance_phi5,
      label="NegBin(φ=5)", linewidth=2)
plot!(predicted_range, negbin_distance_phi20,
      label="NegBin(φ=20)", linewidth=2)
plot!(predicted_range, gaussian_distance,
      label="Gaussian(σ=3)", linewidth=2, linestyle=:dash)

This reveals that different likelihoods are just different ways of measuring distance:

  • Poisson: Penalises deviations inversely proportional to predicted value
  • Negative Binomial: Similar but allows more flexibility (overdispersion)
  • Gaussian: Constant penalty regardless of scale

Learning points

TipLearning points
  • Parametric likelihoods are pragmatic choices rather than mechanistic truths: we pick Poisson or negative binomial for convenience, not because they describe how reporting really works
  • The Poisson assumption that variance equals the mean is testable, and surveillance data routinely breaks it
  • A negative log-likelihood is a distance function, so choosing an observation model is choosing how to measure the gap between prediction and data
  • An observation model that is too tight makes the posterior overconfident. Loosening it widens the intervals honestly and moves the mean, but not towards the days the model fits worst: a mean that is wrong for structural reasons is not fixed by the observation model
TipExercise: Which observation model for which system?

The choice is not abstract. For each of these surveillance systems, say which observation model you would start with and what feature of the system drives the answer.

  1. A sentinel network of GP practices reporting influenza-like illness weekly, with stable participation and no weekend effect.
  2. Notifications of a rare disease, where a single case triggers an investigation that often finds several more in the same household.
  3. Deaths in a national registry, complete but reported with a delay that varies between one and three weeks.
  4. A new pathogen where testing capacity tripled part-way through the outbreak.
TipSolution

  1. Poisson is defensible. Stable reporting, no obvious clustering, and cases arriving independently is roughly what Poisson assumes. Check the variance against the mean anyway rather than assuming.

  2. Negative binomial. Case-finding around an index case produces exactly the clustering that breaks the independence assumption, and the visible symptom is variance far above the mean.

  3. Neither, on its own. The problem here is not the spread but the timing: a case counted this week may belong to three weeks ago. No observation model fixes that. You need a delay distribution between the event and its report, which is a change to the model rather than to the likelihood family.

  4. Neither, again. Tripling testing changes the proportion of infections you observe partway through, so a constant reporting fraction \(\rho\) is wrong. You need \(\rho\) to vary with time. Fitting a fixed \(\rho\) here will make the model infer a change in transmission that never happened.

The general point: 1 and 2 are observation-model choices, 3 and 4 look like them and are not. Asking “is this about how much noise, or about what the data mean?” separates the two.
